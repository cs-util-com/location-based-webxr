Notes

Technique. The cloud shapes are a baked 2D height field: for every point of a world-anchored cloud map it gives the top height (domes with a small billow texture) and a flat base at 1.2 km that only rises at thin edges. Each cloud pixel first finds where its ray enters that shape (a short relief search plus a refinement), then marches only a few hundred metres into the cloud from that point and lights every sample as a volume: density, a light march toward the sun through the same height field, a two-lobe phase function with multiple-scattering octaves, a powder term and sky-lit ambient that darkens toward the base. All sample positions are fixed functions of the ray, so there is no noise to hide and no frame history: every frame is computed from scratch and a still camera gives an identical image. The sea, sky and haze are the glitter-and-haze page's shader, unchanged except that its cloud shadows now come from the new cloud field.

What changed from round 1, and why. The raymarched page jittered because it hid its noise by averaging over frames, and it was slow because it marched the whole cloud layer. Here the march starts at the cloud surface found from the height field and covers only a few hundred metres, so very few steps suffice and nothing needs averaging. The relief page's shapes are kept but lit as a volume instead of as a surface, so the clouds stay solid with flat bases seen from below. Silhouettes are antialiased without jitter: the soft outer layer of a cloud grows with the pixel footprint, and each volume sample averages that layer over its own length, so a ray that crosses it between two samples makes no bands. The search reads a blurred map, whose domes are lower than the sharp ones, so it accepts a small gap that grows with the blur: rays that graze a real dome top are no longer dropped, which removed the stair-stepped edges seen from below the deck. A near-miss check catches rays that dip into a dome between two search samples, and where a ray only grazes a near cloud the search continues to the cloud behind it (a graze that adds nothing does not count as one of the two clouds). Also new: a free camera, an honest frame meter with a GPU timer, and a Measure button.

  • Low tier: canvas at 0.6 of CSS pixels, clouds at 0.75 of that, upsampled with a fixed Catmull-Rom filter clamped to the nearest texels (no ringing, no jitter). 10 search steps, 3 refinements, 3 volume samples, 2 light samples, 2 scattering octaves, up to 2 clouds per pixel, 1 sea-shadow tap, 1024 texel cloud map, 5 wave octaves.
  • High tier: canvas at device pixels (at most 2x), clouds at 0.6 of that, same upsample, 22 search steps, 4 refinements, 5 volume samples, 4 light samples, 3 octaves, up to 2 clouds per pixel, 3 sea-shadow taps, 2048 texel cloud map, 8 wave octaves, faint lens ghost.
  • Measured on this device: press Measure.

Measured cost (Measure button plus the GPU timer; integrated laptop GPU through Chromium with Direct3D, headless, on a machine shared with other work, so the spread is partly load). Per frame:

  • High, 1440x900 (clouds 864x540): GPU timer 6.4 ms at 10 km, 5.8 ms at 3 km, 6.1 ms at 30 km (6.7-7.0 ms during Measure); forced-sync 7.6-17 ms. That is about 7x headroom against 50 ms and 2.5x against 16.7 ms.
  • Low, 1440x900 (canvas 864x540, clouds 648x405): GPU timer 2.0 ms at 10 km and 3 km, 1.7 ms at 30 km (2.9-3.6 ms during Measure): about 3x cheaper than high.
  • Phone size 390x844 at 3x pixel density, 10 km: high (canvas 780x1688, clouds 468x1013) GPU 6.2-7.2 ms; low (canvas 234x506, clouds 176x380) GPU 0.9-1.0 ms.
  • The smooth edges below the deck (the search tolerance and the averaged soft layer) cost about 0.3 ms on high and 0.1 ms on low, measured back to back in one session.
  • Software WebGL (CPU), 390x844 at 1x, 10 km, low: about 0.3 s per frame, and the meter agrees (3.2 fps, median 283 ms). A low-end phone GPU is several times slower than this laptop GPU, which puts low at roughly 4-8 ms and high at 25-40 ms per frame (an estimate, not measured on a phone).
  • Headless browsers run at a fixed 60 Hz whatever the load, so the Measure slope often sees every point at the display cap and gives only an upper bound; the GPU timer and forced-sync numbers are the ones to trust there. On a phone the slope method is the intended one.

Controls. Drag to orbit around the point ahead, right-drag or two fingers to pan, wheel or pinch to fly in and out, W A S D to move, Q and E to sink and climb. The camera stays between 100 m and 60 km. Time keeps running while you move.

Self-check against the reference and the review.

  • Stable in time: yes, by construction and measured. With the camera and the time held, two frames are bit-identical, and moving the camera away and back gives the identical frame again (no history). With the waves held, one 60 Hz frame of cloud drift changes the image by about 0.1-0.3 of a colour step on average, with 0.02-0.2% of pixels changing by more than 8 steps: smooth drift, no flicker. The waves and glitter move as on the glitter-and-haze page.
  • The tops are rounder and softer than the reference's cauliflower: the billow texture is a few hundred metres deep and the volume lighting smooths it. From 3 km the clouds read as soft pillows. Sharper crowns need a finer billow map or a second detail octave (one more lookup per sample).
  • A height field cannot overhang and has one top and one base per point; there are no towers leaning over, no holes through a cloud, and no wisps. Light that reaches a point from more than about a kilometre away along the sun is not counted, so long shadows of one cloud on the next are missing.
  • The bright rim on clouds in front of the sun is a tuned term on the thin outer layer, not the result of the light march; at low sun the backlit faces are a little too bright and creamy toward the sun.
  • Edges are smooth now from above and below at both tiers, but a few silhouette pixels still show small dark notches where a ray grazes a near dome, and thin bright streaks remain along the far lower edges of clouds seen from below (thinner than one cloud texel, so the low tier draws them as short dashes). On a high-density phone the low tier's cloud buffer is about a seventh of the screen's pixels per axis, so edges there are visibly soft.
  • Each pixel shows at most two clouds; a third one behind a grazed edge shows the sea instead. Far clouds are prefiltered and flatten toward the horizon, which suits the haze but loses the far small clouds of the reference.
  • The frame meter once showed a median of 0 ms: pausing and resuming the page within one frame started a second animation loop, so every display frame was counted twice. There is now exactly one loop, the window restarts after a pause, the readout says how many frames it rests on, and "at display cap" needs the frame rate to match the display as well. In headless browsers the Measure slope mostly sees the display cap; the GPU timer is the better number there.
