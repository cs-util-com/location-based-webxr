Steps 1 to 4 · at your desk

It starts with one printed code.

Print a poster and hang it where your walk begins. A visitor scans it with the phone camera: the tour opens in the browser, nothing to install, and the phone knows the spot it stands on.

Step 5 · on site, with your phone

Walk your route. Tap where it happened.

You scan your own poster, then walk. Wherever something belongs, tap: a station drops onto that spot, and you add a note, a photo or a 3D pin. No code, no map to draw.

Your visitors

This is what your visitors see.

Their own phone becomes the window. Arrows lead the way, and your notes and photos wait at the real spots. Outside the screen, the park stays a park.

Visitors need an Android phone with Chrome.

Step 6 · save it

Yours, and nothing to install.

The whole tour is one file in storage you already have: Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, GitHub or your own server. No sign-up, no account, no app.

Common questions

Which phones work?

Creating on site and walking a tour in AR need an Android phone with Chrome. On a computer or another phone you can prepare a tour: storage, link and posters.

Do I need to write code?

No. You walk the route with your phone and tap where things belong. If you want to fine-tune it later, there is an editor for the computer.

Where is my tour kept?

In your own storage: Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, GitHub or your own server. There is no sign-up and no account.

Does it work without signal?

Once a tour has loaded on the phone, it keeps working without signal.

About this prototype

Direction

The site's developer landing, forked for makers: the same dusk 3D world, glass panels and scroll story, with every chapter rewritten for someone who wants to make a tour, ending on "Make your tour" and the first setup step.

Why it should work for this audience

The 3D world already explains "things appear at real places through a phone" better than words; what failed a maker was only the copy (a positioning problem, code, "Fork"). Keeping the look makes the two pages one site, so a maker who lands on the developer page and a developer who lands here both recognise where they are.

Kept from the developer page

  • The dusk palette (teal base, cream text, crimson accent), film grain, vignette, system font.
  • The fixed 3D world behind a single scroller; the hero veil that lifts as you scroll; glass panels alternating left and right; chapter dots.
  • The world itself, rebuilt smaller: S-shaped path, dark tree silhouettes, low sun, skyline, teal person, red pins, blue AR content, violet labels, the phone frame as a window, the ruin with its ghost building.
  • Colour-coded words that match what is on screen, fading in as a panel rises.
  • The hero's structure: H1, a line under it, dash bullets, a collapsible box with a monospace summary, "Scroll to walk in"; the closing chapter's one red button, card grid, FAQ, link row.

Changed

  • Every chapter's copy: the accuracy, fusion and "offline" library claims became the maker's steps (desk, site, visitors, save).
  • The code snippet box became the six-step recipe, same box and summary style.
  • "Fork & start building" became "Make your tour", which opens step 1 in place; the eight demo cards became four maker paths; the developer page is one card and one link.
  • The AR line under the H1 and in the last chapter, which changes with the device.
  • The palette button became the AR simulation switch; the phone frame now masks the AR content so it is visible only through the screen.

Cut for the budget

anime.js (scroll position drives the camera directly), the bloom and vignette post effect (vignette is CSS now), shadow maps, five palettes, portal, parkour, satellites, birds and the easter eggs. Lambert materials instead of standard ones.

Load-bearing details

  • Phone portrait: panels sit 21vh into a 130vh chapter, and the camera's view offset pushes the 3D subject 20% down, so copy and subject share the screen (12% puts the phone under the panel, 28% cuts it at the bottom). Landscape and desktop shift the subject 18% away from the panel side.
  • The phone frame masks the AR content (arrows, pin, label, photo): outside its screen the park stays plain. This is the page's one idea; keep it.
  • The hero shows H1, the AR line, the sub and "Make your tour now" on a 390x844 screen without scrolling (on phones the links move above the recipe box). At 360x740 the links fall below the fold.
  • The AR line always says Android with Chrome; the second half changes with the device.
  • Paragraph and list margins in the panels are set here, not inherited: a port into the app's reset styles will lose them.

Cost (desktop machine, not a phone)

Page script 11 kB gzip, three.js 0.185.1 188 kB gzip (requested only after the first paint): 200 kB of the 250 kB budget. Measured at 390x844 on a loaded desktop, not a phone: 5 s idle, no frame over 17 ms; 5 s scroll through the whole page, one 83 ms frame in a cold browser (none in warm runs); with the CPU slowed 4 times, worst frame 33 ms. The 3D starts in steps (build about 40 ms, shaders compiled up front about 60 ms), none of them a long task in a warm browser.

Known weaknesses

  • Close to the clay-story variant in idea (a 3D story for makers); it differs in staying on the developer page's look and order.
  • Dark and dramatic: the paper look of the current setup page is gone.
  • Blurred glass panels repaint over a moving canvas, the costliest part on a phone.
  • The setup start is a stub: step 1 only.
  • On a phone the first screen is text only; the 3D world shows from the first scroll (as on the developer page, whose veil this keeps).
  • The code in the desktop hand-off is a drawn illustration, not a real code.

Parameters it rests on

Chapter height 130vh, portrait panel offset 21vh, view offset 20% (portrait) and 18% (landscape), scroll damping 6 per second, pixel ratio cap 1.5.
