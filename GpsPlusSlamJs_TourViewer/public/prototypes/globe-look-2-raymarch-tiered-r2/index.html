Notes

Technique. A spherical cloud shell (bases 1.25 km, tops up to 3.05 km) is raymarched in its own half-resolution pass: a baked 2D weather map of clustered cumulus domes thresholds a tiling 3D Perlin-Worley shape noise, a height gradient gives flat bases and rounded tops, and a short light march with a two-lobe phase function, multiple-scattering octaves and a powder term lights it. Every frame is computed from scratch: rays start at a fixed blue-noise offset per pixel and nothing is carried over from earlier frames. A full-resolution pass draws the sea, sky and haze of the glitter-and-haze variant unchanged (Cox-Munk glint split by pixel size, Chapman single-scattering haze with the warm horizon band) and lays the clouds over it through a small 3x3 tent filter.

What changed from round 1, and why.

  • The jitter had one main cause. Every frame the cloud rays were shifted by a different sub-pixel offset, and the history was then looked up at that same shifted position. So each frame's accumulated picture was resampled half a cloud pixel away from the last one, and the error added up like a random walk: the whole cloud layer wobbled by about one cloud pixel (two to three screen pixels at tier low) several times a second. A per-frame change of the ray start offset, plus history rejections at cloud edges, added flicker on top. Now there is no sub-pixel jitter, no frame-to-frame change of the offset and no history at all; the grain of the fixed offset is removed by the tent filter instead. With time frozen, two frames are identical to the bit; with time running, they differ only by the drift.
  • Two smaller causes of popping went too. The empty-space skip lengthened the next step whenever a sample found no cloud, so a cloud drifting past one sample moved every later step and the whole pixel popped; the skip now follows a coarse, smoothly filtered coverage map and slides continuously. The far stand-in for distant clouds now fades in instead of switching on. Far samples use mipmaps of the noise and the weather map (filtered by the step length, too), and far sea shadows use the coverage map, so distant clouds do not sparkle.
  • How it was checked (Playwright, laptop GPU, 844x390, the clock fed through requestAnimationFrame so both rounds see the same simulated time). Clock frozen, 1.5 s apart: round 1 changes 7.8 % of the pixels by more than 8 of 255 (mean 2.1), all along cloud edges and shading gradients, the signature of a moving image; this page: 0 pixels, identical to the bit. One frame of running time: round 1 8-9 % (as much as its 1.5 s of drift), this page 0.1 % at low and 0.01 % at high. 1.5 s of running time: 11-12 %, thin outlines on the cloud edges that the drift moved, and the waves. One frame of a 2 deg/s orbit: 1.2 % at low, 0.35 % at high. A pose reached by orbiting and the same pose rendered fresh: identical (nothing is carried over).
  • The sea, sky, sun and haze are the glitter-and-haze variant's shader, ported as it is; only the cloud shadow now comes from this page's cloud field, and the waves stay on the sea when the camera pans. The clouds are hazed with the same aerial perspective, so far clouds sink into the same horizon band.
  • The free camera can look up at the clouds from below, which the fixed views never did: the haze in front of a cloud above the camera overflowed (black and white speckle) until climbing rays got their own form of the haze segment.
  • Free camera, an honest frame meter with a Measure button, and a cheaper low tier (below).

Tiers.

  • low: canvas at 0.75 of CSS pixels (as round 1), clouds at half of that, up to 24 primary steps (about 14 across the slab), 1 light sample, 2 scattering octaves, no erosion detail, sea shadows from the coverage map only, 5 wave octaves, no lens ghost. One cloud target instead of round 1's two ping-pong pairs. Optional ?clouds=0.75 for sharper clouds.
  • high: canvas at device pixels (at most 1.5x), clouds at half, up to 96 primary steps (about 56 across the slab), 5 light samples, erosion detail near the camera, 3 octaves, 2 shadow samples, 8 wave octaves, faint lens ghost.
  • Both tiers skip the sea and sky shading where a cloud is opaque, and the sun halo away from the sun.

Measured with the Measure button: the whole frame is drawn 1, 2, 4 and 8 times per displayed frame (doubling on to 64x while the display cap hides the cost), the GPU is waited for at the end of each frame, and the cost of one frame is the slope of the frame time against the repetitions, ignoring points held at the cap. The GPU timer, where offered, is shown beside it.

  • Software WebGL (headless, a loaded desktop CPU, only ratios count), 390x844: low about 65-76 ms per frame over the three views, high about 266 ms (3.5-4x). Round 1's low tier on the same set-up: 200-280 ms per displayed frame against 83-150 ms here, so about twice as cheap at the same resolution.
  • Laptop GPU (integrated, D3D11), 390x844 at 3x device pixels: low at most 0.4 ms (GPU timer 0.3-0.7 ms), high 2.7 ms (timer 2.4-4.4 ms); 1440x900: low 1.1 ms (timer 2.2), high 4.3 ms (timer 7.9). The integrated GPU changes its clock with load, so repeated runs vary by up to 2x; a low-end phone should be roughly 10-20x slower, so low should sit near 5-10 ms there and high well above the 16.7 ms budget.
  • Noise bake at load: 0.5-1.5 s on the main thread.

Live on this device: press Measure.

Controls. Drag to orbit around the point in the middle of the view; right-drag or two fingers to pan; wheel or pinch to fly in and out; W A S D to move, Q and E to fly down and up. The camera stays between about 100 m and 60 km.
