AR tours, on your phone, on site

You know a story about a place. Let people see it there.

You have a story to tell, or something to show. Here are the tools to put it on the spot, so your visitors experience it in the real world, through their phone.

Make the tour yourself, without programming. Walk there once with your phone and leave your words and pictures where they belong.

How it goes

Your story, told in one walk.

Here is the whole of it, start to finish. Each picture shows what your phone, and later your visitor's phone, would show at that moment.

  1. A story you know

    It starts with something you know.

    The old mill at the edge of the park turned for a hundred years. You know when it was built and what the stream drove, and somewhere you have a photo of it from 1910.

    People walk past it every day and know none of this. You could tell them, right there, at the mill.

  2. On site

    Walk there with your phone.

    Open the page on your phone and go to the mill. Stand where the story happened and hold the phone up. A ring on the ground shows the spot. Tap Place here, and a numbered station drops onto it.

    You do not need anything printed for this. Placing works anywhere along your walk.

  3. Words and pictures

    Add what you know.

    Type a title and a few lines. Add the old photo. They stay on that spot, standing in the air above the station, the way a sign would.

    Then walk on to the next place that has a story, and do the same there. The words can be changed later, at a computer.

  4. When it is ready

    Hang a code where the walk begins.

    At the end of your walk the tour is saved. Print its code on a sheet of paper and hang it where the walk starts: on a gate, a post, a noticeboard.

    There is no hurry. You print it once the tour is ready, not before.

  5. Your visitors

    They scan it, and your stations appear.

    Someone stops at the noticeboard and points their phone at the code. The tour opens in the browser. There is nothing to install.

    They hold the phone up, and your station appears on the spot where you stood, with your words and your photo. Then the next one, as they walk.

And at the mill they read what you wrote, beside the photo you kept. That is the picture at the top of this page.

Other stories, other places

What would you tell?

Ideas, if you want them. Each is a tour like Mill Park: things on real spots, seen through a phone.

  • The tower stands again

    You look after a castle ruin. A visitor holds up the phone at the old stump, and the tower that fell stands there again, as it was.

  • A birthday treasure hunt

    You hide six clues in the park. Each question floats where the last answer pointed, and the last one floats over the cake.

  • Paintings in empty shop windows

    You paint, and the street has empty shops. Through the phone each window holds one of your paintings, for whoever stops to look.

  • More about every painting

    You run a small museum. Indoors, beside a painting or an object on show, the phone tells what the label leaves out: the sketch beneath it, the story of the sitter.

  • A sculpture path

    You show sculptures in the open air. Beside each one stands the artist's first sketch, and a line on how the work came about.

  • The pond life trail

    You teach a school class. At each stop on the bank, the photo of what the children found there floats above the water.

  • A walk as a gift

    You have shared years with someone. You make them a walk to the places of your story: the bench where you met, the café, the bridge, each with a photo from then.

  • The club's training loop

    You coach a running club. At each post on the forest loop, the drawing of the next exercise stands beside it, with how many to do.

About this prototype

Direction

The maker's story, told to the reader in words: you know a story about a place, you walk there with your phone, you add words and pictures, you hang a code where the walk begins, and visitors scan it and your stations appear. Each part of the story is a short section of text with one drawn picture beside it (a drawn place for the first, a drawn phone screen of the real step for the others). Then the setup as sections one under another, then other ideas.

Why it should work for this audience

  • A guide, a teacher or a museum person recognises their own situation in the first sentence ("you know a story about a place") before any tool is named.
  • The words carry the page: a reader who never looks at a picture still learns the whole flow, in the order it is done. The pictures confirm what the words just said and show what the phone really looks like at that moment.
  • Nothing moves while the reader reads: a picture plays its short entry once, when its part of the story arrives, and then holds still.
  • The setup repeats the story's order and points back to it ("the walk in the story above"), so the story and the steps tell one order; printing and hanging are marked "when ready".

Load-bearing details (check a build against these)

  • Order: way in for visitors (a one-line band), the reason ("You know a story about a place"), the visitor's view in a drawn phone, the three ease lines, five story parts, a closing line with both buttons, six setup sections, eight ideas.
  • First screen at 390x844: kicker, headline, the reason, "Make the tour yourself, without programming", the Android line and both buttons, and the top third of the visitor's phone.
  • Story parts are stacked: on a phone the text comes first and the picture under it; from 900 px wide and on a phone held sideways the text stands on the left and the picture on the right. A dotted line (the walk) runs down the left through one round mark per part; the mark sits beside the part's small heading.
  • Story text is large for reading: 17.5 px on a phone, 19 px on a desktop, line height about 1.55, at most 31 em wide; headings in the serif at 25 to 38 px.
  • Each picture plays once. Stacked on a phone, it starts when 60 % of it is in view: by then its text has scrolled above it (the text ends about 290 px above the bottom of an 844 px screen). Side by side, it starts only when 95 % of it is in view, which is also when the part's whole text is on screen, so it plays as the reader arrives, for 2 to 3.3 s. Only one picture moves at a time (an earlier one still playing is put straight to its end); "Show again" under the picture replays it. The hero phone plays its entry once and then sways once, small (4 px sideways, 1.3 px up and down, 0.24 degrees, 4 s); a mouse or a sideways drag moves it gently.
  • Text inside the drawn phones has a 13 px base size: at 390 px wide the card text is 12.2 px, the card title 14 px, labels and sheet text 11.4 to 12 px; only the clock, the small upper-case tags and the photo caption are smaller (9.4 to 9.8 px).
  • The place and the AR marks are separate layers in every screen; the first part's picture is the same drawn place at full colour, with the old photo and a written note laid on it.
  • Paragraph and list margins are zeroed and every gap is set explicitly (12 px between heading and text, 10 px between paragraphs, 20 px above a picture on a phone, 52 to 76 px between parts).
  • Reduced motion: no entry, no sway, no "Show again"; every picture shows its final state.

Technique

Layered SVG and HTML with CSS transforms; the drawn place and the drawn phone are those of the earlier drawn-phone page, reused. No WebGL, no canvas, no library, so the page itself is the no-WebGL floor.

Measured cost (desktop machine under heavy other load, not a phone)

  • JS: inline only, about 19 kB raw, 6.6 kB gzip; no CDN script; a run with canvas switched off asked for 0 contexts. The whole page is about 103 kB raw, 28 kB gzip.
  • Frames at 390x844 over 5 s each (hero entry and sway, a story part playing, a scroll through the page), two runs each with the CPU slowed 1, 4 and 6 times: at 1x p99 17 to 67 ms with 0 to 3 frames over 50 ms per 5 s; at 4x and 6x p99 83 to 400 ms. The earlier drawn-phone page, measured right after it on the same machine, gave the same picture (1x p99 17 to 183 ms, 4x and 6x p99 50 to 2183 ms), so these numbers show the machine's load more than the page. A first page in a freshly started browser has one stall of about 1.2 s (browser warm-up).

Known weaknesses

  • The most reading of any page: about seven screens on a 390x844 phone from the top to the setup. A reader who wants the steps at once has "Make your tour" at the top.
  • The pictures play only once by themselves; a reader who scrolls past quickly sees them in their final state (which is the point, but the motion is easy to miss).
  • On a desktop the story parts leave white space under the shorter texts.
  • The fake codes are patterns, not real codes.

Parameters it rests on

  • Base text size inside the phones: 13 px (?fs=12 / 13 / 14). At 14 the text card covers the photo in the "add" picture on a 360 to 390 px phone; at 12 the card text falls to about 11 px.
  • Hero sway: 4 px (?sway=0 / 4 / 6 / 9).
  • How the pictures move (?play=once / tap / none): once by default; tap keeps every picture still until "Show me"; none never moves.
  • When a picture plays: stacked at 60 % in view (?seen=0.4 / 0.6 / 0.8; at 0.4 it starts while the last lines of its text are still at the bottom of the screen); side by side at 95 % (?side=0.6 / 0.8 / 0.95; at 0.8 or less it starts before its text has fully come on screen, on a desktop and a phone held sideways).
