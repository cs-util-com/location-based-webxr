Notes

Controls. Drag to orbit, right-drag (or Shift-drag, or two fingers) to pan, wheel or pinch to fly in and out, W A S D to move, Q and E to sink and climb. The camera stays between 100 m and 60 km. The view buttons jump the camera; Reset view returns to the current one.

Technique. One full-screen shader, analytic per pixel, on a spherical Earth, so every term could become a patch on a globe's sea and sky shaders. The sea is unchanged from round 1: wave octaves the pixel resolves are drawn as moving slopes (sharp sparkles near the camera), smaller ones widen the glint lobe (a smooth sheen far away), with Fresnel sky reflection, single-scattering air and marine haze and a bright horizon band. The clouds are one deck at 1.3 to 2.2 km, a height field marched along the ray. New: the cloud field is baked once into two tiling, mipmapped textures (a 51 km detail tile and a 410 km cluster tile), so a march step costs two or three texture reads instead of seven noise evaluations, and every read is pre-filtered to the length of the step that makes it.

What changed from round 1, and why.

  • Combed, hairy streaks on the cloud tops at 3 km, and blocky clouds in the low tier. Cause: 5 to 14 march steps with a fixed per-pixel jitter. At a grazing view each step covers kilometres, so neighbouring pixels landed on different steps, and because the jitter was fixed to the screen while the clouds drifted, the pattern also crawled. Fix: no jitter; steps denser near the entry; the field filtered to the step length, so a step cannot skip a feature it could have seen; secant refinement; one extra halfway sample where a ray skims just above a top.
  • Terraces where the first hit moved from one step to the next. Cause: the filter scale was chosen per step, so the surface itself changed between neighbouring pixels. Fix: the scale is a smooth function of distance.
  • See-through lower half of every near cloud. Cause: the soft edge was a fixed band of coverage, which up close is hundreds of metres wide. Fix: near clouds get an edge about 120 m wide and an opaque side wall; far clouds keep round 1's soft edge, which is what the 10 and 30 km views look like.
  • Smooth snow-dune shading up close. Cause: normals taken over 300 m. Fix: the normal scale now follows the pixel size (100 to 300 m), with soft cauliflower lumps and darker creases in the shading and small lumps in the shape itself.
  • Far clouds thinning out. Filtering removes octaves; round 1 replaced each unresolved octave by a constant above its mean, so the far deck was denser. The difference is added back per filter scale, so the deck has the same coverage at every distance.
  • A green-tinted lens ring over the sea is now faint and near white, and the sun's halo is dimmed where a cloud covers the sun.
  • New for the free camera: the deck is marched from above, inside or below it (flat bases from below, a white-out inside a cloud), the haze is computed for points above the camera too, the sea reflects the cloud bases above it, and the altitude along the ray is computed in a form that does not lose half a metre to rounding.
  • Low tier: 0.6 of CSS pixels (steps down to 0.4 when the 95th percentile frame is over 40 ms), 5 wave octaves, 20 march steps and 3 refinements, 2 lump octaves, no cloud self-shadow, no lens ring.
  • High tier: device pixels (at most 2x), 8 wave octaves, 36 steps and 4 refinements, 3 lump octaves, a self-shadow sample toward the sun, faint lens ring.

The frame meter. A browser cannot draw faster than the display, so on a 60 Hz phone every page reads 16.7 ms however cheap it is; that is what "at display cap" means. All frame numbers come from the same frame intervals of the last 2 s: fps is the frame count over that window, so it always equals 1000 over the mean; the median and the 95th percentile ignore a few long stalls (a tab switch, a screenshot) and the mean does not, so the readout also counts the long frames (over 1.5 display intervals) and names the longest. "At display cap" is shown only when the median and the fps both sit at the refresh rate. The GPU line comes from the GPU timer where the browser offers one. Measure draws every frame 1, 2, 4 and 8 times (more if all of those still sit at the cap) and takes the cost of one frame from the slope; the result is compared with a 50 ms and a 16.7 ms budget.

Measured on a laptop GPU (integrated, Chromium with Direct3D), 1440x900: high about 8.3 ms per frame (6x headroom against 50 ms, 2x against 16.7 ms) in the 10 and 3 km views; low about 1.5 to 3.5 ms (12x to 24x and 4x to 8x). The same GPU drew round 1 at about 21 to 25 ms in high and 4 to 7 ms in low, so round 2 is cheaper. Headless software WebGL (no GPU, 390x844) is far slower and noisy: about 0.4 s per frame in low and 0.6 s in high, useful only as a ratio. A low-end phone is several times slower than this laptop; press Measure on it for its own number. Live here: press Measure.

Self-check. This page's strength is the sea and the haze; its clouds are a simple deck, and the question of how close clouds should look is answered by the other pages in this gallery. Up close the tops are a little brighter and the sky-lit sides a little darker than in round 1. The 10 and 30 km views keep round 1's look, with a field of the same statistics but a different pattern. At 3 km the streaks, blocks, crawling and see-through feet are gone, but the clouds are still height-field mounds: no overhangs, no light through thin parts, so close clouds read more like soft snow banks than cumulus towers, and seen from below their sides look like smooth bright sails. Grazing rays that run along a cloud's foot can still show a faint dotted band, and the low tier still has small steps on some silhouettes. A volume (a weather map with a short density march) would fix the near look at about 3 to 6 times this cost. No crepuscular rays, no cloud reflection beyond the bases, no real bloom, and the detail field repeats every 51 km (broken up by the 410 km cluster field). Far glitter has no single sparkles, by design.
