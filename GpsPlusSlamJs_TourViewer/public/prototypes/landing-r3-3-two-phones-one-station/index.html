AR tours, on site, on your phone

You have a story to tell, or something to show.

Turn it into a tour. Your visitors see your words and pictures in the real world, right at the spot, through their phone's camera. You make it yourself, without programming.

About this prototype

Direction

One station, shown twice side by side (one above the other on a phone): your phone while you make it, and a visitor's phone at the same spot, later. Scrolling takes both phones through the five steps of making a tour together, so "what you do" and "what they see" are one picture.

Why it should work for this audience

  • The question a first-time maker has is "if I do this, what will people see?". Each step answers it at once, in the phone next to it.
  • The same place in both phones, drawn the same way, makes "fixed to the spot" visible without the word.
  • The last step makes the two screens identical: the end of the story is a picture, not a sentence.

Load-bearing details (check a build against these)

  • The phones are held sideways in the drawing. That is what lets two of them stack on a phone screen with the text inside still readable; upright phones would need about twice the height.
  • Phone portrait: the two phones stack in a sticky band at the top (about 440 px at 390x844); the step texts scroll in the space below. From 700 px wide they sit side by side, and each step's two lines sit under their own phone.
  • Everything inside a phone is sized in em of the screen's width (1em = % of it, about px on a 390 px phone), so it scales with the phone and never reflows differently from the drawing.
  • Motion: each step plays one short entry (up to about 2.5 s) in both phones at once, with the same timing, then holds still. Nothing loops, nothing moves on load, nothing moves outside the phones. Reduced motion: every step shows its final state.
  • Palette and shapes of the earlier 3D route page (paper, ink, one route red; faceted low-poly hills, mill, wheel and oak), drawn flat in SVG. No WebGL, no canvas, no library.
  • Setup: six sections with rules between them, not boxes; a short line each, more on opening; printing and hanging are marked "when ready" and come after the walk.
  • Paragraph and list margins are zeroed and every gap set explicitly, so a port into the design system keeps them.

Measured cost (desktop machine under other load, not a phone)

  • JS: inline only, about 8 kB raw, 3.1 kB gzip; no CDN script, no WebGL, no canvas. The whole page is about 19 kB gzip.
  • Frames at 390x844, two runs each at 1x, 4x and 6x CPU throttling, over a 5 s idle, the entries of steps 2 to 4 and a 5 s scroll through the page: the entries stayed at 16.8 ms unthrottled (worst 45 ms at 4x, 88 to 114 ms once per run at 6x). Unthrottled scrolling had one long frame per run (0.5 and 0.75 s) that did not come back at 4x or 6x. A second set, taken with the machine at 94 % load from other work, had frames of 0.3 to 1 s everywhere, including the idle where nothing on the page moves, so those are the machine's, not the page's. Desktop Chromium, not a phone; a phone measurement is still owed.
  • In-phone text at 390 px wide: smallest text 12.0 px, card title 13.8 px (11.0 px on a 360 px phone).

Known weaknesses

  • The sideways phones are a drawing choice: a real maker probably holds the phone upright while placing.
  • On a phone the two phones take about half the screen while the steps are read; the step texts have the lower half.
  • Steps 2 and 3 show the visitor's phone before the code is hung (step 4): it shows what a visitor will see, not the order a visitor lives it in.
  • The fake codes are patterns, not real codes. The scene is a drawing of a made-up park, not a camera image.

Parameters it rests on

  • In-phone text size ?k= (percent of the screen width per em). Compared at 390 px: 3.5 gives 10.8 px, no better than the earlier drawn phone; 3.9 (the default) gives 12.0 px with nothing overlapping; 4.3 gives 13.2 px but the visitor's card runs into the top bar. From about 4.2 up the card has to move.
  • Scroll per step ?cap= (svh per step). 40 shortens the story by about 600 px on a phone, but on a 360x740 phone and on a phone held sideways the next step's text arrives before the current one has left; 52 (the default) keeps one step in view; 65 adds about 660 px of scrolling for nothing.
  • Animation length: ?sp= (a factor on every step's entry; 1 is about 2.5 s at most).
  • ?ar=1 / ?ar=0 fake the device's AR support; the toggle at the top does the same.
