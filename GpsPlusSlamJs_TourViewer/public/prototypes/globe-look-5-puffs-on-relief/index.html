Notes

Technique. One full-screen pass draws sky, sea and the cloud deck; a second pass draws a small set of lit puffs on top. The deck is a height field (cloud thickness above one flat base at 1.2 km) baked once into a tiling, world-anchored texture from a list of domes, and each pixel marches its ray through the deck, refines the hit and shades it with a short self-shadow march toward the sun. The same dome list places the puffs: one on each crown and a ring on the shoulders, where a dome's outline is seen against the sky or sea. Each frame the puff's vertex shader reads the deck height under it, so it always sits on the surface it belongs to, and sinks into it where it would only add a bump inside the outline (facing the camera), where it would be smaller than a few pixels, or where the deck is thin.

What changed from the first round and why.

  • The relief deck was invisible from below (the march only ran from the top down). It now runs from the base up too, so from under the deck the flat base is solid and dark, brighter where the cloud is thin, and lit from the sides by the sky.
  • The puffs used to be re-sorted once a second, which changed the picture once a second. Now nothing is ever sorted: puff cores are opaque and write their round depth, so they hide each other and meet the deck correctly in any order; only their soft outer fringe is blended, in a fixed order, and a wrong order there is a faint, still difference, never a jump.
  • The deck no longer jitters its march per pixel (the first round's fine white fuzz on outlines that crawled as clouds drifted): the samples are fixed, the far deck reads a blurrier level of the texture where its samples are far apart, and the cloud outline's opacity is a continuous function of how close the ray passes, so drift moves it smoothly.
  • Free camera, an honest frame meter with a Measure button, and the water study below.

Tiers. Low: 0.6 of CSS pixels (steps down to 0.4 when frames run long), 18 deck steps, 3 refinements, 3 self-shadow taps, 1 sea-shadow tap, 1024 texel deck map, puffs only within 32 km and only when at least 2.5 pixels wide (big and medium clouds, fewer puffs per cloud). High: up to 1.5 device pixels, 44 steps, 5 refinements, 7 shadow taps, 3 sea-shadow taps, 2048 texel map, puffs out to 90 km and down to 1.5 pixels, faint lens ghost.

Water study (?water=1|4|mix, the Water buttons; sky and haze above the water are the same for all three):

  • 1: the first variant's sea as it was: wave octaves the pixel can resolve are drawn as moving slopes, smaller ones widen the glint lobe, so the sun path goes from sharp sparkles near to a smooth sheen far.
  • 4: the fourth variant's sea: three swell octaves plus a random count of glinting facets per pixel-sized cell (more sun sparkle); its cells re-draw their count 2.2 times a second, which is a hard twinkle.
  • Mix: the first variant's sea with fewer explicit octaves (3 low, 5 high instead of 5 and 8), plus the fourth variant's sparkle count on the glint term only, cross-faded in time so cells twinkle smoothly instead of switching.

Measured (Measure button, which renders the whole frame 1, 2, 4 and 8 times per displayed frame and takes the slope): press Measure.

Against 50 ms the low tier fits 15 times or more on this laptop and the high tier 3.5 times. Software rendering (SwiftShader, no GPU, 384x240 low and 640x400 high) takes about 40 to 100 ms for the sea alone, 0.8 s per frame for low with clouds and 2.4 s for high; its Measure slopes are noise and say nothing. On the GPU the slope and the timer disagree by up to 2x for the cloud frames (the browser overlaps frames, the timer counts idle gaps); read the true cost as lying between them. The three seas differ by less than the run-to-run noise (about 1 ms at this size): mix is not measurably cheaper than 1, because its saved wave octaves are spent on the sparkle count. The sea costs about a tenth of a cloud frame, so the choice between them is a look choice.

On a real globe the water shading would run only on water pixels: the surface shader already knows a land or sea class per pixel (from a land/sea mask tile, or a water mask derived from the surface texture's colour), and blends this sea term in by that mask with a soft coastline. The glitter is faded by distance and by view angle exactly as here (the footprint split already turns it into a smooth sheen far away), and switched off entirely beyond the distance where the sheen is below one display level, which also bounds its cost. Inland water gets the same term with a calmer wind.

Stability, how it was checked. With the loop paused and the clock held, the page was rendered and screenshotted, and the screenshots were compared pixel by pixel. The same time twice: no difference. One frame (1/30 s) apart: 0.1 to 0.3% of pixels change by more than 24 of 255 levels. 1.5 s apart: 10 to 15% with clouds, 2 to 4% for the sea alone. That is the drift: clouds move fast on purpose so the motion shows from 10 km. To catch popping as opposed to drift, three frames were taken in a row and a pixel counted as a pop when the middle one leaves the range of its two neighbours by more than 16 levels: 0.08 to 0.12% of pixels with clouds (single pixels at self-shadow creases and where a puff cuts into the deck), 0 for the sea alone. During a slow orbit (0.1 degree per frame) 0.06 to 0.7% pop, mostly sea glitter, which moves with the view by nature. Nothing is sorted, so there is no once-a-second change any more.

Self-check, bluntly. From 10 and 30 km the combination works: the deck gives the flat, shadowed bodies and the puffs only lift the outlines, so far fields read as cumulus, not as a crowd of balls. Close up (3 km) it breaks: the puffs read as separate soft balls placed on the relief, lit flatter than the deck beside them, and a near puff seen against its own cloud looks pasted on. The near relief smears into streaks at grazing angles. From below the bases are now solid and flat, but their edges are smooth lens shapes and the brightening where the cloud is thin is weak. The high tier is not a phone setting (9 to 14 ms of GPU on a laptop, almost all of it the deck march, not the puffs); the low tier is about 2.5 to 3.2 ms there, so a mid-range phone should hold 30 to 60 fps, which is an estimate, not a measurement. About one pixel in a thousand still flickers at shadow creases (measured above). The water mix is the first variant's look with smoother sparkle, not a saving.
