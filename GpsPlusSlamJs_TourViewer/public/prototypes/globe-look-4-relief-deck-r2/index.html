Notes: relief cloud deck, round 2

Controls. Drag to look around, right-drag or two fingers to pan, wheel or pinch to fly in and out, W A S D to move and Q E to sink and climb (Shift is faster). The camera stays between 100 m and 60 km.

Technique. One full-screen pass over an analytic sphere. The cloud layer is a volume with a flat base: everything between the base height and a baked, tiling thickness map (clustered unions of domes plus a billow detail map, with gradients and mipmaps). Each pixel marches its ray through the part of the cloud slab it crosses, finds where it enters the volume (through a flank, a top, or the flat base), refines the entry, and then takes a few short taps further along the ray to measure how much cloud it passes through: that chord sets the opacity, so thin edges are translucent and every deep path is solid. Tops and flanks are lit from the stored gradient with a short sun shadow march; the flat base gets its own sky-lit, grey-blue shading with light leaking through thin parts.

What changed from round 1, and why.

  • Solid undersides. Round 1 set a hit's opacity from the column thickness, so the lower part of every flank, where the column is thin, faded out: clouds were transparent at the bottom. Opacity now comes from the path length through the cloud, and the flat base is part of the volume, so clouds seen from the side or from below are solid and have a flat, darker base.
  • Camera above, inside and below the deck. Above: the march runs from where the ray enters the top of the slab to where it meets the base height (or leaves the slab again). Inside the slab: it starts at the camera; if the camera is inside a cloud, the cloud is drawn as fog up to the point where the ray leaves it, then the march carries on. Below: it starts where the ray crosses the base height going up; if there is cloud there, that is a hit on the flat base, otherwise the ray climbs through the gap and can still hit the flank of a neighbouring cloud.
  • Soft base edges from below. The cloud's outline sits 80 m up the map's ramp from zero to the dome height: a contour right at zero followed the texel grid of the blurred map and showed as stair steps and straight cut-off lines along every base. Thin parts count as less dense, so bases fade out where the cloud thins. The blur level is a smooth function of distance (no stepped bands in the gaps). Light leaks through a thin base only where the path to the sun is clear, and the lowest few hundred metres of a flank seen from below take the base's grey, so base and flank meet without a pale rim.
  • Stable in time. The per-pixel random march offset is gone (with drifting clouds it crawled as a white fuzz on every edge). The march is deterministic, and the cloud map is sampled at a blur level that matches the march step as well as the pixel size, so far clouds blur into the haze band instead of turning into crumbly noise.
  • Free camera, a "Below" preset, an honest frame meter with a Measure button, and ?clouds=0 to measure the water alone.

Tiers.

  • Low: 20 march steps, 5 refinement steps, 2 chord taps, 3 shadow taps, 1 sea-shadow tap, one glint level, 1024 texel cloud map, render scale 0.6 of CSS pixels (adapts 0.45-0.8 to keep frames near 30 ms).
  • High: 44 march steps, 7 refinement steps, 4 chord taps, 7 shadow taps, 3 sea-shadow taps, two blended glint levels, 2048 texel cloud map, render scale up to 1.5 device pixels.

The frame meter. The readout shows frames per second and the median and p95 frame interval over the last 2 s, and says "at display cap" when the median sits within 1 ms of the display interval (measured with empty frames at start). A 60 Hz screen never shows less than 16.7 ms, whatever the page costs. Where the browser offers a GPU timer, the GPU time per frame is shown as well. Measure renders the whole frame 1, 2, 4 and 8 times per shown frame (and keeps doubling up to 64 while the work is still small), about 2 s each, and waits for the GPU after each shown frame; one frame's cost is the slope of that time against the repetitions, so fixed overheads cancel. The GPU timer and the slope of the plain display interval (over the points not held at the cap) are shown next to it as cross-checks.

Measured cost (Measure button, laptop with an integrated Intel Iris Xe GPU, Chromium with GPU, the machine shared with other work):

  • High, 1440x900 at 1 device px: 10 km about 12.0 ms per frame (GPU timer 11.2 ms), 3 km 7.0 ms (6.9). The Below view (looking toward the sun from a gap between clouds, much of the frame grazing the deck near the horizon) about 8 ms (GPU timer 8.0); looking straight into the underside of the big cluster, where most rays stop at the base at once, 2.9 ms. Repeated runs on this shared machine vary by up to 2 ms. With ?clouds=0 the water alone costs 1.45 ms (GPU timer 1.5 ms) at 10 km and 3 km, so the clouds are about 80-90% of those frames.
  • Low, 390x844 (234x506 px): GPU timer 0.38 ms at 10 km, 0.35 ms at 3 km and 0.18 ms Below; water alone 0.13 ms. Low at 1440x900 (864x540 px): GPU timer 1.5 ms, water alone 0.5 ms, so in the low tier the water is about a third. At these small costs the slope reads higher (0.4-4.7 ms), because the fixed cost of each extra draw call dominates; the GPU timer is the better number there.
  • Not measured on a phone. A low-end phone GPU is roughly 10-20 times slower than this one, which would put the low tier at about 4-8 ms and the high tier at a phone's full resolution well over the 50 ms budget.
  • Startup: the cloud maps bake once in about 0.1-0.7 s.

Stability check. Two screenshots 1.5 s apart with the page panels hidden: with time frozen (?freeze=1) they are identical, so nothing changes from frame to frame by itself (no random march offset, a fixed dither). With time running, the water alone changes as waves and glints move (8 500-16 000 isolated single-pixel changes, the glitter twinkling by design); with clouds the isolated changes drop to 2 500-6 700 (clouds cover water), and the cloud differences are whole surfaces moving, not specks. A slow orbit (1 degree per second) adds no specks either.

Self-check against the reference and the review.

  • Undersides: solid now from every side, with a flat grey-blue base from below and darker lower flanks from the side. The base is still too even: real cumulus bases are ragged and show darker cores and lighter edges more strongly. Looking toward the sun the flanks are a fairly flat grey with only a thin bright rim; the domes read as lens shapes more than cauliflowers from below. The clusters are large (several km), so under one the sky overhead is closed.
  • Inside a cloud the page draws an even grey fog up to where the ray leaves the cloud: good enough to fly through, not a look (no density variation, a hard line at the base height).
  • Still pillows: thin edges are now translucent and shadow sides lighter, but the tops remain smooth dome unions with billows of 120-530 m; no wisps, no light through the body of a cloud. Real softness needs a volume.
  • Hard, aliased silhouettes: without the random offset there is no fuzz, but edges are single-sample, and seen from below near the horizon far bases show thin ragged streaks. Temporal accumulation with reprojection (one history buffer) would fix both.
  • Far edges: the deck now blurs into a smooth band toward the horizon instead of white crumbs; far clouds are slightly lower and softer than in round 1 as a result.
  • Unchanged from round 1: the haze band is thinner and less golden than in the reference, the glitter path narrower near the horizon, the small clouds at 30 km too uniform, the cloud map tiles every 64 km, and the sky and haze are hand-fitted rather than a physical atmosphere. Clouds drift about ten times faster than a real wind, on purpose, and the billows slide over the domes at their own speed.
