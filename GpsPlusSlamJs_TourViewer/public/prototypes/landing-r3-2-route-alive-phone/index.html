Make your tour

Six steps, one after another.

Start on any computer. The walk happens on site, with an Android phone with Chrome. Nothing is printed while you build: the code comes last, when the tour is ready. Open a step to read more.

  1. 1

    Where your tour's file is stored

    Your tour is one small file, kept in storage you already have.On any computer

    Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, GitHub or your own server. The setup gives you an empty starter tour to put there.

    The file stays yours. Once a visitor's phone has loaded the tour, it keeps working without signal.

  2. 2

    The link that opens your tour

    Paste the file's share link once. It is what your code will open.On any computer

    The page checks the link right away. When you change the tour later, you replace the file, and the link and the code stay the same.

  3. 3

    Walk the route and place your stations

    At each spot: stand there, tap to place, add a few lines and a picture.On site, with an Android phone with ChromeOn site: this phone can do it

    This is the walk the story above shows. You can start right away, without a printed code: placing works anywhere along the walk.

    Words can be changed later, on a computer.

  4. 4

    Save the tour and put the file back

    When you finish the walk, the tour is saved on your phone. It replaces the file in your storage.From your phone or a computer

    The page shows how, for the storage you chose in step 1. The link does not change.

  5. 5

    Print the code When ready

    A PDF with your tour's code, printed at actual size.On any computer, then a printer

    Printing can come after the walk, whenever the tour is ready. Each page has a 10 cm bar to check the size.

  6. 6

    Hang it where the walk begins When ready

    Flat on a wall or a post, at about chest height.On site

    Pick a spot a phone can see squarely from about a metre away. Visitors scan it there to open the tour.

About this prototype

Direction

The earlier route page's low-poly model park and its self-drawing route stay the hero. A phone joins the model for the making side: the story is told in scroll steps (a story you know, walk there, add words and pictures, hang a code, visitors see it), and at each step either the route moves or the phone acts, never both. The phone's screen is the same model park seen at eye height from where the person in the model stands.

Why it should work for this audience

  • The model park reads at a glance as a place and the numbered pins as an order; the phone shows what a person standing in that place actually does with it.
  • The person in the model, a dashed line to the phone and a pale wedge of view on the ground tie the two pictures together: this phone belongs to that person, looking that way.
  • The station placed in the making steps is the one the visitor saw at the start, and the last step walks the finished route, so the story closes where it began.

Load-bearing details (check a build against these)

  • Order: way in for visitors (one line at the top), motivation and the route drawing itself, what a visitor sees, the five making steps (plus one step that walks the rest of the route), the setup as six sections on a dotted route, ideas.
  • One moving thing at a time: a step waits until the camera has arrived, then plays its own short sequence once; the camera never moves while the phone acts, and the phone is put away before the camera moves on. Text sits in its own grid cell and never moves while the picture does.
  • Scroll: the stage is sticky; each step takes % of the screen height of scrolling. Going back shows a step finished, without replaying it; scrolling past a step skips its sequence.
  • Framing: the overview fits the whole park into the picture cell's real camera frustum; the close views fit the start of the route into the part of the cell left of the phone, so the phone never covers the person, the pin or the code.
  • Phone: about of the picture's width on a portrait phone, height-limited elsewhere; text inside it has a 13 px base (at 390 px wide the card title is 14 px and its body 12.2 px; the smallest text is the photo caption at 10.4 px and the clock at 9.6 px); a screen narrower than 158 px drops the card body, the share sheet's second line and the station dots.
  • The phone does not sway: its screen is drawn by the 3D scene underneath it, and one moving thing at a time is the rule here.
  • Paper palette and serif headings of the earlier route page; route red only for the route, pins, the maker and the primary button.
  • Reduced motion: every step shows its finished picture at once, the camera jumps, nothing plays. Without WebGL: the same steps as flat drawings, with drawn screens in the phone.

Known weaknesses

  • The story is long on a phone: eight scroll steps before the setup. "Skip to the steps" is always on screen.
  • The eye-height view is a model, not a camera image: it says "this is the place" more than "this is AR".
  • three.js takes most of the 250 kB budget.
  • The fake codes are patterns, not real codes.
  • Frame times could not be measured on a quiet machine (see the cost above); on a phone the camera moves and the walking steps are the frames to watch.
  • Below about 158 px of phone screen (a 360 px phone, or one held sideways) the card shows its title only.

Parameters it rests on

  • Scroll per step: ?beat= (55 / 70 / 90, default 70).
  • Camera move: ?cam= seconds (0.8 / 1.1 / 1.6, default 1.1); act speed ?speed=.
  • Phone width on a portrait phone: ?ph= (0.42 / 0.47 / 0.52, default 0.47).
  • Text base inside the phone: ?fs= (12 / 13 / 14, default 13).
  • Close-view direction ?az2= (-12 / 4 / 22, default 4), overview ?az= (default 72).
  • ?ar=1 / ?ar=0 fake the device's AR support; ?gl=0 forces the drawings without 3D.
